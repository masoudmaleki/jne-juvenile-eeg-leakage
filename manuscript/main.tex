\title{High accuracy without demonstrated generalisation: data leakage and recording confounds in resting-state EEG classification of juvenile offenders}

\author{Mesut Melek$^{1}$\orcid{0000-0002-7152-7788} and Negin Melek$^{2,*}$\orcid{0000-0001-5297-5545}}

\keywords{EEG, data leakage, cross-validation, juvenile offenders, confounds, resting state, generalisation}

\begin{abstract}
\textit{Objective.} Machine-learning studies report high accuracy for separating offenders from controls with brain data, but such results can arise from data leakage and from confounds of recruitment and recording. We asked how much separability survives correct validation in an open resting-state electroencephalography (EEG) dataset of juvenile offenders, and whether it generalises.
\textit{Approach.} We analysed 128-channel resting-state EEG of 74 male juvenile offenders, labelled as two subgroups (sg and sg2), and 66 controls; group was confounded with recording period, substance use, schooling, socioeconomic background and age. We audited the data and their precomputed features, quantified leakage with naive and nested subject-wise pipelines, tested one hypothesis designated as primary (alpha reactivity), quantified the null result of prespecified region-of-interest features and, in exploratory analyses, tested whether a channel-level separation of sg from controls transferred to sg2. All decisions were recorded in a time-stamped analysis log.
\textit{Main results.} The precomputed features came from a partly different cohort, and most of their statistics were computed from three values. A naive pipeline, built here to illustrate the error, reached a subject-level area under the ROC curve (AUC) of 0.89--0.91 and still 0.79--0.85 with shuffled labels; nested subject-wise validation gave 0.66--0.73. Alpha reactivity did not differ ($p = 0.395$), and region-of-interest features did not separate the groups (AUC 0.54, 95\% confidence interval 0.44--0.63). Channel-level features separated sg from controls (AUC 0.64--0.75; largest effects at frontopolar sites), but none of five models showed evidence of transfer to sg2 (AUC 0.55--0.59; upper confidence limits 0.66--0.70).
\textit{Significance.} Leakage markedly inflated the apparent accuracy in this dataset, and the remaining separation could neither be shown to generalise to a second offender subgroup nor be separated from recording confounds. EEG classification studies of forensic groups should split data by participant, report permutation nulls and confidence intervals, and test transfer across subgroups or sites.
\end{abstract}

\begin{figure}
  \centering

  \caption{Exploratory channel-level analyses: sg separated from the controls, but no model showed evidence of transfer to sg2. (a) Subject-level AUC for sg versus controls (100 identity-verified participants; SVM in nested subject-wise cross-validation) with log absolute power on all segments and with relative power on all segments, eyes-open segments, eyes-closed segments, eyes-closed segments without the C block, and the long eyes-closed block. Whiskers show the 2.5--97.5\% range across 20 repeats, not CIs (subject-bootstrap CIs in table~\ref{tab:TableS4}); grey bars show the 95\% range of the shuffled-label null. Filled markers: $p < 0.05$ (exploratory, unadjusted; with 200 permutations the smallest attainable value is $p \le 0.005$). The C block comprises 32 BioSemi electrodes over frontopolar, anterior frontal and right frontal sites; lateral frontal electrodes outside it remain. (b) Transfer: models trained on sg versus controls, tested on held-out controls versus sg2 ($n = 24$) within each fold. Thick lines: 2.5--97.5\% range across repeats; thin lines: subject-bootstrap 95\% CIs. No model showed evidence of transfer (AUC 0.55--0.59; $p = 0.104$--0.289); significance would have required an AUC above 0.59--0.64. (c) Descriptive: relative delta at C-block sites and relative alpha at A-block sites in the eyes-closed segments ($2 \times 50$~s), median and interquartile range.}
